What was the biggest dinosaur ever, and how much evidence actually stands behind the record?

04 · Update

Dossier update, November 2026. We went back over every figure in the episode with the sources and the latest data. Point by point, here is where the numbers stand today.

  • The video said Supersaurus was about 39 m long. The data show the main peer-reviewed estimate is 33–34 m and 35–40 tonnes (Lovelace et al. 2007); the 39 m figure comes from later reassessments not published in a journal.
  • The video called 96.4 tonnes the highest value ever given to Argentinosaurus. The data show it is the highest point estimate among the sources we used, not the highest ever proposed: Benson et al. 2014 give 90 tonnes with a 95% interval reaching 124 tonnes.
  • The video presented 50.1 to 84.5 tonnes for Bustingorrytitan as a range of published estimates. The data show it is a single estimate, 67.3 tonnes, with its own ±25.6% prediction error, calculated from a reconstructed femur.
  • The video said Tyrannosaurus rex weighed about 9 tonnes. The data show typical adult estimates of 6–8 tonnes, with the largest specimen, Sue, perhaps around 9.5 tonnes (Hutchinson et al. 2011).
  • The video gave Argentinosaurus a length of about 36 m. The data show no neck or tail has been found, and published length estimates run from about 30 to about 40 m.
  • The video said Patagotitan is known from more than a hundred bones. The data show Carballido et al. 2017 report at least six individuals, but we could not confirm that bone count in their main text.
  • The video gave Patagotitan's mass as 42 to 77.6 tonnes. The data show the main text of Carballido et al. 2017 gives 77 tonnes as the top volumetric value, and the 42-tonne figure comes from a 2020 revision we could read only as an abstract.
  • The video said that Maraapunisaurus's preserved spine stands about 1.5 m tall and that a 2006 estimate reached 122 tonnes. The data show 1.5 m is Cope's reported measurement, which some researchers consider a likely typo for 1.05 m, and we could not verify the 122 tonnes in the primary source.
05 · The Verdict

We know that a handful of Patagonian titanosaurs, led by Argentinosaurus and Patagotitan, were among the heaviest land animals ever, and that Patagotitan and Dreadnoughtus are the best documented of them. What we cannot say is which one was heaviest: the gaps between them are smaller than the error bars and method differences of single estimates, and the record holder is known from about 5.1% of a skeleton. Maraapunisaurus might once have rivaled them all, but its only bone is lost, and estimates of its size have shrunk from about 58 m to about 30–32 m.
