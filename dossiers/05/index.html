01 · The Question

Why can't we see Chicxulub, the impact crater linked to the end of the dinosaurs?

A clay layer about 1 centimeter thick at Gubbio, Italy, led scientists in 1980 to predict an asteroid about 10 km wide, before any matching crater was known. The crater, Chicxulub, was identified in 1991, buried under about 1 km of younger carbonate sediments.

04 · Update

Dossier update, November 2026. We went back over every figure in the episode with the sources and the latest data. Point by point, here is where the numbers stand today.

  • The video said the Gubbio clay held more than 30 times the iridium of the surrounding rock and gave exact concentration values. The data show about 30 times; the exact values could not be checked against the full text of the 1980 study.
  • The video presented the Yucatán anomaly as a find made around the time of the Alvarez study. The data show Pemex had recorded the gravity anomaly since the 1950s, and Penfield and Camargo presented it as a possible impact structure in 1981.
  • The video said roughly 17,000 meteorites reach the ground each year. The data show the estimate by Evatt and colleagues is about 17,600 falls heavier than 50 grams per year, and a single fall can produce several meteorites.
  • The video said one genus, the bird Telmatornis, made it to the other side of the boundary. The data show it crosses the line only because some of its records carry an age range spanning the boundary, a dating uncertainty rather than proof of survival; the birds that did survive were the ancestors of modern birds.
  • The video presented the 15–25% crater survival figure and Chicxulub's 70% share of impactor mass as results of our own analysis. The sources show both are estimates published by Schmieder and Kring in 2020.
05 · The Verdict

The episode's core story holds up: a centimeter-scale, iridium-rich clay layer led to a 10 ± 4 km impactor prediction in 1980, and the Chicxulub crater, about 180 km across and dated to 66.05 million years ago, was identified as an impact crater in 1991. Chicxulub is hard to see because it is buried under about a kilometer of carbonate sediments, and Earth keeps only a small fraction of its craters. A few details needed correcting: the Yucatán anomaly was known from oil surveys since the 1950s, the yearly figure of about 17,600 counts falls rather than individual meteorites, and the dataset's lone 'surviving' genus reflects dating uncertainty, not proven survival.
